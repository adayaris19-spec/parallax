# Parallax shorts: rules

Draft. Each rule comes from an answer the owner gave; the date and topic are noted. Rules marked "settled" come from section 5 of `SUMMARY.md`.

## 0. Carried over (settled)

- Narrated: Kokoro `am_michael`, speed 1.12.
- Subtitles: one phrase at a time, sentence case, no colour highlight. No labels, kickers, stamps or dark cards.
- No beat-driven music. The score ducks under the voice.
- One visual journey per video. Graphics sit over footage, never on black.
- Length 20–25 s.
- No clip used twice across a goal's videos. No cartoons, text or logos in footage.
- Every claim from the goal's facts or the post's sources, qualifiers kept. No donate or follow ask.
- What makes a story work (confirmed Nov 21): a real event arc with real dates and a punchline, as in the "Moon by 2020" Reel.

## 1. Purpose and feeling

Confirmed Nov 21.

- **Arc: stakes, then momentum.** Every video runs hook → build → peak → end.
  - Hook: the stake or obstacle, as one concrete fact (a number, a date or a named thing). Spoken by 1.0 s; finished by 4.0 s.
  - Build: why that makes the goal hard. One or two lines.
  - Peak: the real advance, with its qualifier. Starts between 50% and 70% of the running time.
  - End: one forward-looking line. The qualifier or catch may come before it, never after it.
- **Feeling to test for:** tension, then release, then "I want this". Scorecard question: "Did the peak feel like a pay-off to the hook?"
- **Viewer action: keep watching.** The video is judged on completion and on the next video starting, not on clicks.
  - No spoken or written ask of any kind (donate, follow, share, "learn more").
  - No end card. The last narrated word ends 0.5–1.5 s before the final frame, and the last shot holds to the end.
  - No Parallax logo or tagline inside the video; the app already shows whose it is.
  - The first frame must work as the next video's opening: a full picture, never a fade from black.

## 2. Subject matter

Draft confirmed Nov 21; three rules added Nov 22 after the first moving clip.

- **Always moving.** No still real-life photo is held on screen as a shot. (Owner: "I don't want videos to be static real life images.")
- **The vision (owner, Nov 21): original made imagery at NASA's level of detail, not NASA's clips re-used.** NASA's computer-made pictures (for example a Milky Way view no camera could take) are the model for detail and visual quality. Parallax makes its own in that style. Real footage may be mixed in for goals where it fits. This corrects section 6.7 of `SUMMARY.md`, which read the references as "real photographs".
  - Constraint still standing: no paid AI image or video tools. How to make such imagery for free is the Phase 4 question; nothing is promised until a test render is shown.
- **Lines with no footage of their own** (a lab result, a number, a policy): a thin-type graphic over real moving footage. Draft conditions, to confirm:
  - the footage under the graphic is the place or object the story is about, not just the goal's general world;
  - the graphic states the thing the line says (the study, the number, the date).
- **Source facts are checked against the record before a graphic is drawn.** Example: the laser-tile study is dated 2026 by Crossref (Open Ceramics vol. 28, DOI 10.1016/j.oceram.2026.101082, CC BY 4.0); the Nov 21 sample's clipping said 2024.
- **Level (confirmed Nov 21):** more dramatic than NASA's plain data renders (clips B and C of Nov 21). The target is the richer look of NASA's artist illustrations: more colour, atmosphere and depth.
- **Mix (confirmed Nov 21): made by default, real for real events.** Real camera footage appears only under a line about an event that was filmed (a launch, a landing, a field trial). It is never used as generic background.
- **Look reference (confirmed Nov 21):** NASA/JPL artist illustrations; see `reference/look-nasa-artist-concepts.jpg`. The owner accepted all four kinds, so the kind follows the subject:
  - cosmic, vivid colour (PIA22085) for deep-space and physics subjects;
  - landscape with cinematic light (PIA21423) for places;
  - people and hardware in a place (PIA23302) for "the goal achieved";
  - clean orbital realism (PIA23690) for planets and spacecraft.
  - What they share, and what ours must have: one clear light source, deep shadows, real surface texture, a sense of scale, no flat or untextured shapes.
- **Visual richness (owner, Nov 21, on the first 3D still):** "anywhere you look in that image, you're gonna be looking at it for at least 5 seconds." Detail must fill the frame, not sit in one object.
  - Draft measure: split the 1080×1920 frame into a 6×10 grid of 180×192 px cells. At most 15% of cells may be "empty" (local contrast below a threshold to be set from the NASA references). The first 3D still fails: its sky and its shadows are empty.
  - No region of pure black larger than 2% of the frame. Shadows keep visible detail. Exception (Nov 22): sky that a real camera would record as black; see "Only what a real camera could record".
  - No surface with a single flat colour: every surface shows variation at three scales (large shapes, mid texture, fine grain).
  - Named failure to avoid: "nice thing that AI made". A viewer with no stake should be held, not just pleased.
- **One picture, not layers (owner, Nov 21, on the Moon still):** "everything individually looks good, but when they all blend together it looks very separate… the Earth is good by itself, but against the background it just looks fake."
  - Every element in a frame goes through one shared lens pass: the same softness, the same grain, the same colour grade and the same black level.
  - Bright objects affect what is around them: glare lifts the sky next to them and dims faint detail there, and background light wraps a few pixels over object edges.
  - No two layers may be exposed as if for different cameras (for example a long-exposure star field behind a daylight-exposed planet at full strength).
- **Judged in motion (owner, Nov 21):** stills are not enough; every look is approved as a moving shot.
- **Something is going on in every shot (owner, Nov 22, on the moving Moon clip):** "right now it's just three objects, the Earth, the Moon's surface and the background… moving just vertically up or down"; "more elements, more individual things"; "it can still move around slowly".
  - Four kinds of "going on", all accepted; which one "really depends on the clip":
    1. things at work: hardware and people doing something in a real place;
    2. a camera that travels: it goes somewhere, and near things pass close to the lens;
    3. the science happening: the shot shows the process itself;
    4. many small details: small things moving all over a calm shot.
  - Draft measures, to confirm on the next sample:
    - ~~at least 6 separately identifiable things in frame~~ (withdrawn Nov 22: see "Every picture has a job");
    - at least 2 of them move on their own, not only because the camera moves;
    - depth is visible: something sits close to the camera, and near things cross the frame at least 4 times faster than the farthest thing (in the Moon clip everything moved the same way at about 1 px per frame);
    - the camera may be slow. Speed is not the fix.
  - Named failure to avoid: "layers sliding", meaning the whole picture moving in one direction as two or three flat layers. A long lens from far away causes it.
- **Only what a real camera could record (confirmed Nov 22).** Parallax is science-based; the owner called the Moon clip "not even scientifically right".
  - True places, sizes, distances and light direction.
  - One exposure for the whole frame. No bright stars beside sunlit ground or a sunlit planet. This withdraws the brightened star field of Nov 21.
  - Speed is real, or an honest time-lapse in which everything in the frame speeds up by the same factor. A camera that flies faster than a real spacecraft while the rest stays in real time is not allowed (the Nov 22 Moon clip flew 2.7 times too fast).
  - Consequence for richness: where a real camera shows a black sky, the subject has to fill the frame. Draft limit, to set on the next sample: black sky covers at most 15% of the frame.
  - Each made shot records what in it is measured (data, published models) and what is modelled (anything we added), with sources.
- **Things that do not exist yet (confirmed Nov 22): published designs, or our own built from published plans; the mix depends on the goal.**
  - First choice: real hardware and official prototypes or designs (for example NASA's published 3D models), in real places with real light.
  - Our own designs are allowed where no published model exists, when every part follows a published plan: its size, its place, the number of people. The source is recorded per element.
  - The narration says it is the plan, not a photo. No on-screen label (settled).
- **Every picture has a job (owner, Nov 22, on the Moon base clips):** "Every visual needs to have a purpose… delivering the message, showing the progress, demonstrating, catching the engagement, something. It cannot just be there because it's pretty." And: "Right now you just put a bunch of things on the Moon. Is that realistic? Is that what the goal is? That's the biggest thing right now."
  - Order of work: message, then script, then shot sheet, then pictures. No picture is made before the line it serves exists.
  - Each shot serves exactly one script line and shows the thing that line says. Its job is written first, in one sentence: "This shot shows … so the viewer understands …".
  - Swap test: if the shot could sit under a different line, or in another goal's video, without anyone noticing, it fails.
  - Nothing is in frame to fill it. Every object is listed on the shot sheet with its source and the reason it is there.
  - Realistic means true to where the goal really stands. What has not been built is not shown as built. A future thing appears only under a line that says it is planned, and only as the published plan describes it.
  - The Nov 22 Moon base test fails all of this: prototypes from different programmes, placed together at a site, serving no line. It stays in the lab as a test of movement and speed only.
  - This withdraws the Nov 22 draft measure "at least 6 separately identifiable things in frame". Richness has to come from the real subject's own detail, not from added objects.
- **Made 3D stays the star, and the bar is film visual effects (owner, Nov 22, on the first complete video):** "It looks like I did this for a school project." "I still want this to be the main star of the thing… Interstellar… and so many other films or projects. Aim that high bar standard. Really push the limits." (The owner means computer-made film effects.)
  - Clear: "I want it to look really clear. Right now it's low-key pixelated." No grain or softness is added. Frames are rendered larger than delivery size and scaled down. Any visible blockiness at 100% is a fail.
  - Detail: hardware seen close comes from its maker's own high-detail model. A model that looks simple at the size it has in frame is not used at that size.
  - Earth: "The Earth is really bad in the background." When Earth is in frame it is exposed so that oceans and clouds show, and it is at least 100 px across (draft; 113 px in the orbiter shot). Otherwise it is left out. Never a white disc.
  - Movement: "It's not even moving. It's just going up or down." In every made shot the camera travels, and at least one thing in the scene moves on its own. A tripod time-lapse in which only the sky slides is a fail as a main shot.
  - Variety: "The visuals are boring me because they're just really repeated." No subject appears in more than one shot of a video.
- **Explainer mix (owner, Nov 22):** "You could mix in some articles in there and some other things to differentiate." Asked which kinds: "depending on the situation all could work"; asked about the look: "I still want 3D with more detail, but you can mix it with explainer mix."
  - Allowed elements: a headline card (outlet, headline, date); the real page with the key sentence highlighted; a big number or quote with its source named; maps and charts drawn from the data.
  - Each element comes from one of the goal's own sources. A page is shown only when its source is free to show (space agencies, open-access papers); any other source gets a headline card.
  - An element sits over a moving picture, never over a still or a flat background.
  - Made 3D carries at least 60% of the running time (draft).
- **Restart (owner, Nov 22, on the orbiter shot):** "This 3-second clip took you 4 hours." "You're not really applying the feedback I'm giving you. Every single time I say change it, it's actually the same." "Go back to the inspirations… restart from the beginning." "You're an AI. You can be an artist."
  - What was the same every time: a slow, grey Moon under black sky with one computer-made object. The references (the "Moon by 2020" Reel, NASA's artist illustrations, NASA's Instagram) are vivid, dramatic and about events.
  - Mix (owner's answer): "mostly 3D, but we could mix up with real things, and also the articles… filler elements that will also reduce repetitiveness and reduce the waiting time."
    - More than half of the shots are made 3D. Real footage carries real events. Cards, numbers and charts sit on top. Short fillers may reuse a made shot we already have.
  - What is wrong with the pictures (owner picked all four): dull and colourless; nothing happens; looks computer-made; wrong things shown ("show people, missions, the real stakes").
  - Look for the made shots (owner's answer): cinematic. Dramatic light, flare, warm and cool colour, a readable Earth. Places, hardware, dates and facts stay true. This outranks "one plain exposure" wherever the two collide; each collision is listed with the video.
  - Relevance and real footage (owner, Nov 22, on seeing the Artemis II crew footage being gathered): "Make sure whatever you're putting down actually relates to the goal… the Artemis 2 crew, which I don't see how that relates. I don't want to use that much actual real footage. I do still want more AI made. You can use some real footage, but I don't want a discrete difference between the real footage and the other footage."
    - Every shot shows the goal's own subject (for the Moon base: the base, the people there, the pole, its night and its sunlight). A related mission is not the goal.
    - Real footage is optional. It goes in only where it cannot be told apart from the made shots around it in subject, light and grade. No windows, frames or labels that mark it as "real".
  - Time: a whole video's new 3D is rendered in one run of at most 30 minutes. A shot that cannot fit is simplified or replaced.
- **Places and scales (owner, Nov 22, on the first full restart video):** "This is honestly not that bad." "It's still not visually rich." "It's low-key the same clip a lot of times." "Right now it's the same moon, just either zoomed in or zoomed out." "It can still look more realistic. Especially when it's spinning around: very different objects moving on their own. It's not continuous." "Make those changes more drastic." "Make sure what you're putting is physically correct, and makes sense… not AI fluff." "The text and the voice is really good."
  - A video uses at least 4 different kinds of place or scale. For the Moon base: the whole Moon from space, Earth, the pole from above, the ridge at ground level, hardware, people.
  - No kind carries more than 3 shots, and never 3 in a row.
  - At least 2 shots carry a strong colour that is not grey or black (Earth's blue, lamp light, gold foil, a chart).
  - A clip the owner has seen before is used again only where it shows something no new shot does, and each reuse is listed with the video (our reading of "the same clip a lot of times"; to confirm).
  - Time-lapse moves in small steps: between two frames nothing lit moves more than about 16 px at 1080×1920. The sunlight sweep that turned the Sun 3–6° per frame is the failure this rule names.
  - Whole-Moon and Earth views are built on photographed maps (NASA's Moon mosaic, the day's satellite picture of Earth), never on a made-up texture.
  - Physically correct stays the test of every picture. The place, the date, the Sun and Earth are real. An exposure that could not record something does not show it (no stars beside sunlit ground).
- **A space film with action (owner, Nov 22, late, on the second restart video):** "Slightly better, but I'm not seeing these big improvements." "All of the clips need to be improved." "The beginning 5 seconds is when you hook someone. If I gave it 5 seconds, I would just scroll past." "The earth spinning too fast, it's too close to the moon. I don't think it's physically right." "All day what you're giving me is the same clip, but slightly different." Asked what was wrong with the Moon, Earth and orbiter shots, the owner picked all four: the pictures themselves, the on-screen graphics, nothing happens, it looks fake. Asked for the direction: **"Space film with action"** (made 3D of the real place, showing events: a landing, people working, a base being built). Asked what the opening should be: "just a visually rich, full, striking video will catch attention."
  - Every shot shows something happening: a vehicle moving, a machine working, a person doing something, a landing. A shot of terrain or a planet alone does not count.
  - The first shot is action from its first frame, with its subject filling at least a third of the frame.
  - The Moon or Earth seen whole from space is not a shot. Earth appears only as the eye would see it from the ground: about 2 degrees across, never enlarged by a long lens, never turning in time-lapse.
  - No thin counters, place labels or source cards over the picture. Subtitles stay. The source of a fact goes in the post's caption.
  - Frames are full: black sky takes at most about a third of the frame in daylight shots.
  - Hardware is NASA's own models where they exist, moving the way the real thing moves (the crew rover at no more than its published 10 km/h; dust thrown in arcs with no air to hold it).
- **Method scrapped (owner, Nov 23, on the action opening):** "You need to scrap this method… this is still not the level of detail that we are aiming for. The physics of this is still wrong. There's literally trail marks before the trail even goes there. It's not even a full reel."
  - Cause and effect in order: nothing shows the result of an action before the action (no tracks ahead of a wheel, no dust before an engine fires, no shadow before its object).
  - What is sent for a verdict is a full video of 20 to 25 s, unless the owner asked for a sample.
  - A production method is kept only if it reaches the detail of the owner's references and makes a full video in the time budget; hand-built 3D rendered on processors did neither.
- **Option B chosen (owner, Nov 23, on the method proof):** "Let's do B, but the movements are not smooth and it doesn't look that realistic, so that needs to be improved. Also, how would option B look for another goal that's not related to space? Keep that in mind. Create this reel now for the Moon base. Go over the requirements. Don't miss anything."
  - Method: agency footage and renderings first; an open AI video model adds motion where only a still exists (`DECISION.md`).
  - Smooth: every shot runs at 24 real frames a second, with no frame shown twice and none skipped. Footage shot at 30 frames a second is played frame by frame (it runs at 80% speed). An AI clip is made at 24 frames a second by the model itself; the 16-frames-a-second sample of Nov 23 is the failure this rule names.
  - Sharp: an AI clip is made at delivery size (1088×1920) from a source picture at least that large. Nothing is enlarged, agency footage included: a 9:16 window of a 3840×2160 frame, never of a 1920×1080 one.
  - Realistic: an AI clip goes in only if it holds up beside the agency footage around it, and only after a frame-by-frame look for shape changes, things appearing from nowhere and effects before their cause.
  - No moving shot is built from a still by a camera push alone.
  - Any goal: the method may not depend on a space agency. Each goal gets its own list of free, rights-clear sources before its first video; for goals with little agency video, real photographs serve as first frames. No identifiable person without their permission.
  - Before a video is sent, it is checked against this file line by line, and every miss is listed with the video.
- **A story in one place (owner, Nov 23, on the option B reel):** "It's getting better. Some of the 3D elements are actually pretty nice, but some of them are way weaker. It's like transitions of astronaut, back to back. It's not actually interesting. It needs to be visually engaging for the audience, because I wouldn't watch this. It's not entertaining."
  - Liked: the glove with the rock (shot 2), the astronaut kneeling at work (4), the base in sunlight (6), the AI-moved walk to the rover (8). Each time the owner added: "Still needs continuity though."
  - Not picked: the astronauts at the base with Earth (1), the small lander (3), the astronaut on the ladder (5), the dark plain with lamps (7).
  - Format (owner's choice): **one night, one place.** One base, one event with a beginning and an end: the Sun leaves, the dark comes, the lamps go on, the Sun returns.
  - What makes the owner keep watching (owner's choice): a story with a payoff, and real sound (radio, breathing in the helmet, machinery, silence when night falls, hits on the cuts). Not chosen: a faster pace, fewer words.
  - Continuity: every shot is the same place in the same state of light as the shot before it, or one step further along the event. No cut to a subject that is not part of that place.
  - The wide view of the place carries the story and comes back; close shots are cut-ins inside it.
  - This replaces "7 to 9 shots, about 2.0–3.5 s each" for a one-place story: a take may run as long as its event needs.
  - Sound is part of the picture: each event on screen that would be heard (a lamp switching, a radio call, breath) has its sound; when the light goes, the score drops away.
  - Draft measures from the first one-place reel (Nov 23, to confirm on the verdict):
    - the wide view holds at least half of the running time (61% in that reel);
    - on the wide view, the night never sits unchanged for more than about 2 s;
    - a lamp comes on only after the dark has reached it;
    - sound effects are made for the reel, never recordings with voices; only sounds a helmet would carry (radio, breath, the suit, knocks felt through the structure); they are loudest in the gaps between sentences, and the narration must still be heard back word for word;
    - subtitles carry a soft dark glow so they read on sunlit ground.
  - Open, for the owner: adding our own night, shadow and lamps to agency footage is a third ingredient beside footage and AI motion. It also runs a shadow in seconds that takes hours, beside a rover at normal speed, against "an honest time-lapse".
- **More variety, sharper words, bigger sound (owner, Nov 23, evening, on the one-place reel):** "It is actually so much better." "The sounds can be more dramatic." "Continuity still has to improve." "The shots are good, they're just repeated. It just needs more diversity within." "Go over the hook sentences and the other sentences, make sure they make sense, it is easy to follow and gets attention." "I think you're starting to understand much better."
  - **Our own night, shadow and lamps on agency footage are accepted,** on the owner's terms: "as long as it looks like it belongs to the space and it is not some fiction thing… as long as it could be real in real life and it is physically correct."
    - Each added thing sits on something that is in the shot and would really be there: a lamp on a hatch or a post, a shadow from the Sun's real side. No added objects.
    - It obeys physics: light direction, cause before effect, and sunlight comes back from the side the dark came from.
    - At full size it cannot be told from the footage around it.
  - **Repetition** (asked what repeats, the owner picked all four: the wide base view, shots from the last reel, too few different shots, astronauts again):
    - no framing appears twice in a video;
    - the widest view of the place carries at most a third of the running time (draft; it was 61%);
    - no shot the owner has seen in an earlier reel is used again (this tightens the Nov 22 rule);
    - at least 7 different pictures in a video, all of the same place and the same event;
    - suited astronauts in at most 2 shots (draft).
  - **Continuity:** asked which cut broke it, the owner picked the cut from the dim blue night to the bright white suit in grey light.
    - Across a cut, the light matches: same colour of light, and mean brightness within a factor of 2 (draft), unless the event itself changes the light in that moment.
  - **Sentences** (the owner chose the "plain and short" wording):
    - the hook is at most 10 words, carries a number anyone can picture ("about fourteen days", not "354 hours"), and is finished by 3.0 s;
    - no sentence over 12 words;
    - the reason is said, not left to guess ("Life support cannot pause.");
    - a word like "it" or "there" has its noun in the sentence before, and the last sentence names the place.
    - The chosen script: "On most of the Moon, night lasts about fourteen days. / Small landers have survived it by mostly shutting down. / A base with people cannot. / Life support cannot pause. / No base with people has yet run through a lunar night. / Some ridges at the south pole get sunlight most of the time. / But even the best go dark for days in some seasons. / NASA has said its astronauts would visit the south pole by 2030."
  - **Sound** (the owner chose "film-style power"): deep booms on the main cuts, a swell that climbs into the moment the light dies, a beat of near silence, then a big swell when the Sun comes back. Not chosen: louder suit sounds, bigger music, real radio voices.
    - This outranks "only sounds a helmet would carry" (draft of the same day).
- **A fuller opening, joins like a film, far more detail (owner, Nov 23, night, on the nine-shot reel):** "The opening visual has to be the most exciting, most eye-catching thing ever. Right now, it's empty. It can still be the same thing, but more details, more things in the sky and the space, more exciting things." "The transitions are like if I'm in school and I made a slideshow and clicked the most basic transition ever. I want smooth transitions, as in a frame can go into another frame without it looking like it was edited, like a movie scene." "I don't necessarily have a problem with the shots. Just differentiate them and make them more rich: the small details that you wouldn't see, but if you really looked at the frame, you're like, oh my god, yeah, that exists. I want an extreme amount of detail."
  - **Opening:** the first shot holds the most in it of any shot in the video. Draft measures: at least five separate things to look at in the first frame (for the Moon base: the Sun, a spacecraft's light, the shadow front, the masts, a rover), something moving in the sky, and no more than a quarter of the frame empty (plain black or plain sky).
  - **Joins:** no plain cut between shots. Shots of the same place are joined by a camera move across the same picture. Other joins are hidden: inside a camera whip, inside a light that fills the frame, or behind something crossing the lens. Draft measure: at each join, no frame shows one picture stop and the next start.
  - **Detail:** every shot carries small real things a second look finds. For our own additions the earlier condition holds: only what would really be there and is physically right. Used so far: catalogue stars once the ground is dark (never beside the Sun's glare), beacons blinking on masts, a spacecraft crossing in orbit, a lander's engine light, lamps that stutter as they strike.
  - Stars are real ones (the HYG catalogue: Hipparcos, Yale, Gliese). Where the source gives no date, the date we assume for the sky is written with the video.
- **One continuous clip (owner, Nov 23, late, on the reel with whips and a flare):** "This is exactly what I didn't want. The transitions are literally what you would see in a school slideshow. I don't want obvious transitions. I just want it to be one continuous clip. I don't want any of these cliché transitions." "The lander is fine, it's acceptable. It just can still be more detailed." "The Earth… be careful of the shadows." "The detail is getting much better, but go through these really fine details, make it look like it's out of a space movie."
  - **No cut and no transition effect of any kind:** no whip, flare, dissolve, wipe or fade. A video is one camera take from the first frame to the last. This withdraws the "hidden joins" of the rule block above and its whip and flare.
  - If a second shot is ever joined on, the join must be one nobody can find; no such join is used until the owner has seen and passed one.
  - **Shadows are checked one by one.** Each shadow has a cause in the picture and falls away from the Sun. The unlit part of the Earth is a shadow too: it hides the stars behind it. (Our reading of "be careful of the shadows"; the reel before this one showed stars through the Earth's dark side.)
  - Added craft are drawn as craft, not as lights: a lander has a body, legs, a lit side and an engine that lights the ground as it comes down.
  - The bar for detail is a space film: small things at work across the frame (people's helmet lamps, a rover far off, beacons, spacecraft overhead), none of them placed where it could not be.
- **More to see in the one take, the place checked, nothing that reads as digital (owner, Nov 23, about 23:00 UTC+1, on the one-take reel):** "The transitions are good now. It's very continuous." "It's boring because it's just the same thing. I'm just watching the camera look left and right." "For example, when the camera looks back, there could be something else there, or just more exciting things." "Make sure you get the logistics right. Is the Earth's angle right? Is the Moon actually that mountainous?" "Some parts still look very digital, AI made. Make everything look polished, like it's out of a sci-fi space movie. I want an extreme amount of detail, very fine details. Some that a human wouldn't even notice."
  - **Passed:** one unbroken take, no cut, no transition effect.
  - **The view changes.** Draft measures:
    - at least three different kinds of view in a video (for the Moon base: the plain with the base, the sky, a person close);
    - the camera comes back to a view only if something new is standing or happening there (a change of light alone counts once);
    - no stretch longer than 7 s in which the only change is the camera moving left or right.
  - **The place is checked before it is shown.** Each video gets a short list of what is right, what was corrected and what is assumed: where the Sun and the Earth sit, the Earth's phase from its angle to the Sun (lit part = (1 − cos angle) / 2), how rough the ground is, how fast things move.
    - A source picture that contradicts its own geometry is corrected, and the correction is written down. First case: NASA's frame shows the Earth about 8% lit with the Sun 21.5° from it; that angle allows about 3.5%.
  - **Nothing reads as digital.** Causes named and removed in this round:
    - a shadow edge like a soft brush stroke (it now follows the ground's relief and is drawn out along the Sun's rays);
    - a sky of separate dots (now NASA's star map: the faint stars and the Milky Way they make up);
    - a perfectly clean, perfectly still picture (now one grain, one lens fall-off and a trace of camera sway over everything, source and additions alike);

- **Length:** 20.0–25.0 s (settled).
